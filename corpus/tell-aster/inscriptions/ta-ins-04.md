# Seal Impressions with Royal Names

## Scope and record history

The edition is based on raking-light photography, hand copy, and direct inspection. Readings are separated
from restorations, damaged signs are marked in the discussion, and palaeographic comparisons are used
cautiously because the local sequence is still developing. Sign forms were checked under changing light angles
because shallow strokes can disappear under frontal illumination. Surface scratches caused by excavation or
weathering are excluded from the hand copy where their sequence can be established.

## Recording and analytical procedure

Transliteration follows the expedition convention of preserving uncertain vowels and marking damaged signs. A
readable sequence is not automatically treated as a personal name; grammar and repeated formulae are
considered in the commentary. Find context is important for historical use of the text, but the inscription
report does not itself infer the full history of the building or grave from which an item came. Those
associations remain in the excavation records.

During archive preparation, drawings, photographs, sample logs, and object registers were reconciled against
the written narrative. Not every recorded observation contributes to the interpretation: colour changes at
baulk edges, isolated body sherds, displaced stones, minor root disturbance, and uncertain joins are retained
because they document the actual evidentiary texture of excavation. Coordinates and measurements were checked
for internal consistency, but values were not normalized merely to make neighbouring records agree. Where the
team used a shorthand description in the field, the expanded wording appears in the discussion while the
original identifier is preserved. This makes the archive suitable for later reanalysis without requiring the
reader to assume that the first interpretation was final.

## Detailed observations

Measurements and line spacing are included because layout sometimes distinguishes labels, ownership marks, and
short accounts. These physical observations also help determine whether fragments belonged to a larger
original object. The archive entry retains minor measurements, negative observations, and rejected joins
because these often matter when a later specialist revisits the material.

## Supplementary observations and limitations

The physical history of each inscribed object was considered alongside the text. Breaks that cut through
strokes help establish whether writing preceded fragmentation, while plaster, soot, or abrasion can indicate
later reuse. Some sherds were written after firing and then incorporated into secondary deposits; others may
have been deliberately retained. These observations matter because a readable text can be chronologically
misleading if its object was reused. The edition therefore keeps textual dating, archaeological dating, and
object history separate unless they independently converge.

Alternative readings are recorded where sign shape or damage allows more than one possibility. The preferred
reading is chosen from stroke order, spacing, grammar, and parallels inside the Tell Aster corpus rather than
from an assumed historical narrative. Personal names receive special caution because short sequences can
resemble common nouns or titles. Numerical signs and measures are transcribed conservatively, and the
commentary notes when a damaged stroke would materially change the quantity. This preserves the possibility of
later re-edition without requiring access to the original object for every question.

Photographic enhancement was used to test readings but never as the sole basis for adding a stroke. The
archive keeps unenhanced reference frames alongside contrast-adjusted study images, and the hand copy records
only marks confirmed on the object or across multiple photographs. Proposed joins between fragments are
described separately from textual restorations. This separation prevents a plausible reading from becoming
circular evidence for a physical join that has not been demonstrated.

## Additional analytical caution

The commentary records not only the preferred transliteration but also why competing readings were rejected.
Stroke depth, direction, spacing, grammatical expectation, and repetition elsewhere in the archive are weighed
together. No historical interpretation is allowed to supply a missing sign simply because it would make a
convenient name or title. This keeps the epigraphic evidence independent from later synthesis.

## Final record-quality note

Line drawings were compared back to the object after the first draft because a clean drawing can make
uncertain strokes appear more definite than they are. Signs whose reading depends on one lighting angle remain
marked as uncertain, even when a plausible word results. The archive also keeps measurements of margins and
line spacing, since layout can help distinguish a label, ownership mark, short account, or longer text
independently of the lexical reading.
