# Trench T8 Stratigraphic Sequence

## Scope and record history

This record was compiled from field notebooks, context sheets, level readings, and the end-of-season trench
matrix. The aim is to preserve the sequence of excavation decisions as well as the later interpretation,
including places where working hypotheses changed as sections were cleaned. Excavation proceeded in shallow
arbitrary spits only where deposits were homogeneous; otherwise stratigraphic boundaries were followed.
Sediment colour, compaction, inclusions, and interfaces were recorded before removal. Particular care was
taken around wall faces and floor edges, because thin resurfacing episodes had been missed in earlier seasons.

## Recording and analytical procedure

Levels were checked against the permanent datum at the start and end of each working day. Small finds were
plotted individually when recovered in situ, while bulk pottery and animal bone were bagged by context.
Flotation samples were taken from sealed lenses and deposits with visible charred material, avoiding exposed
baulk edges. The matrix shows several episodes of cutting and resurfacing rather than a simple stack of
occupation floors. Some deposits that appeared continuous in plan separated clearly in section, especially
where ash lenses pinched out against walls. These relationships were used in the later phasing discussion.

During archive preparation, drawings, photographs, sample logs, and object registers were reconciled against
the written narrative. Not every recorded observation contributes to the interpretation: colour changes at
baulk edges, isolated body sherds, displaced stones, minor root disturbance, and uncertain joins are retained
because they document the actual evidentiary texture of excavation. Coordinates and measurements were checked
for internal consistency, but values were not normalized merely to make neighbouring records agree. Where the
team used a shorthand description in the field, the expanded wording appears in the discussion while the
original identifier is preserved. This makes the archive suitable for later reanalysis without requiring the
reader to assume that the first interpretation was final.

## Detailed observations

Several provisional identifications written in the daily notebook were revised after cleaning. This is normal
field practice: labels such as 'floor', 'collapse', or 'pit fill' describe an early interpretation and are
retained here so that the chain of reasoning remains visible. The archive entry retains minor measurements,
negative observations, and rejected joins because these often matter when a later specialist revisits the
material.

## Interpretive discussion

The present interpretation gives greatest weight to associations that can be checked independently, while
preserving uncertainty where the record does not support finer resolution.

## Supplementary observations and limitations

The trench archive also preserves observations that did not become formal contexts. Thin trample patches,
isolated charcoal flecks, rodent burrows, patches of salt efflorescence, and small concentrations of mudbrick
fragments were sketched when they affected the visibility of interfaces. These notes are useful because the
apparent clarity of a published matrix can obscure how uncertain some boundaries were during excavation.
Pottery buckets from uncertain edges were kept separate until the interface was resolved, and bags judged to
cross a boundary were flagged rather than merged retrospectively. In several places the team returned to
already-cleaned sections after overnight drying changed sediment contrast; those rechecks explain minor
differences between daily notebook descriptions and the final context narrative.

Find recovery was uneven for practical reasons and is discussed rather than hidden. Heavy architectural
collapse was initially excavated with larger tools, whereas floor deposits and grave fills were removed with
finer tools and more systematic sieving. The difference affects the recovery of tiny beads, fish bone, and
chipped-stone debris. Where a concentration appears significant, the report therefore asks whether collection
method could account for part of the pattern. Photographic coverage likewise varies: some surfaces were
recorded in both oblique and vertical views, while short-lived deposits exposed late in the day may have only
a single frame before removal. These limitations are part of the evidentiary record.

## Register extracts and secondary observations

Archive control for TA-EXC-03 included comparison of 20 photographic frames, 20 registered material or sample
entries, and 9 drawing or measurement checks. The reconciliation produced several minor observations that were
retained even though they did not alter the main interpretation: a cluster of fist-sized stones proved too
irregular to define a deliberate paving episode; small pockets of gypsum crystals along a wall face were
recorded as post-depositional and not separated as contexts; and a shallow animal burrow crossed the upper
fill but did not reach the sealed deposit discussed later. These details are deliberately kept in the research
record because later work may give them significance, but none is promoted beyond the strength of the original

## Additional analytical caution

The daily register also records work that produced no spectacular result: cleaning wall faces, checking baulk
sections, rebagging mixed pottery, and revisiting interfaces after changes in moisture. These operations
matter because they explain why some context boundaries are more secure than others. Where the final matrix
differs from the order implied by early diary entries, the report preserves enough procedural detail to
reconstruct when the change occurred.
