# Textile Tools and Household Implements

## Scope and record history

Objects were examined macroscopically and under low magnification, with measurements taken after initial
cleaning. Catalogue identifications distinguish raw material, manufacturing traces, use-wear, and post-
depositional damage. Measurements were taken with digital callipers to 0.1 mm where preservation allowed.
Surface accretions can affect mass, so weights recorded before and after conservation are not assumed to be
directly comparable.

## Recording and analytical procedure

Manufacturing traces include sawing, drilling, abrasion, casting seams, hammering, and polishing. Functional
identifications are strongest when shape and wear agree; typological resemblance alone is treated as
suggestive. Objects from graves and sealed floors are discussed separately from finds in fills. The catalogue
preserves excavation provenance even where later specialist work changed the functional or material
identification.

During archive preparation, drawings, photographs, sample logs, and object registers were reconciled against
the written narrative. Not every recorded observation contributes to the interpretation: colour changes at
baulk edges, isolated body sherds, displaced stones, minor root disturbance, and uncertain joins are retained
because they document the actual evidentiary texture of excavation. Coordinates and measurements were checked
for internal consistency, but values were not normalized merely to make neighbouring records agree. Where the
team used a shorthand description in the field, the expanded wording appears in the discussion while the
original identifier is preserved. This makes the archive suitable for later reanalysis without requiring the
reader to assume that the first interpretation was final.

## Detailed observations

Parallels from other Tell Aster contexts are used to define local groups. External comparisons are
deliberately limited because all chronological conclusions in this fictional archive are meant to be
recoverable from expedition evidence itself. The archive entry retains minor measurements, negative
observations, and rejected joins because these often matter when a later specialist revisits the material.

## Supplementary observations and limitations

Catalogue numbering reflects excavation registration rather than specialist order. Consecutive find numbers
may therefore belong to different materials, trenches, or seasons, while objects studied together can have
widely separated identifiers. The report preserves this structure because it is the link back to field
provenance. Measurements were repeated for objects whose corrosion or accretion changed during treatment, and
the preferred value is identified explicitly where more than one survives in the archive. Fragments thought to
join were tested physically only when their condition allowed; otherwise possible joins remain noted but
unconfirmed.

Functional interpretation draws on traces at several scales. Edge polish can indicate repeated handling, but
similar polish may arise from burial movement; perforation wear may show suspension, yet not whether an object
was worn on the body or hung from equipment. The catalogue therefore distinguishes observation from
interpretation and avoids forcing every fragment into a complete-object typology. Objects recovered together
are listed as associations without assuming they formed a set. This is particularly important in fills, where
material from different episodes can accumulate before deposition.

Spatial clustering was assessed cautiously because find registration practices changed between seasons. In
situ coordinates are preferred when available; otherwise the context remains the smallest defensible spatial
unit. Objects from sieving are not assigned a precise position within a deposit, and concentrations of small
finds are not reconstructed from bag sequence. This distinction matters for household and workshop
interpretations, since a visually impressive group can arise from a large volume of excavated fill rather than
from a discrete ancient activity area.

## Additional analytical caution

Contextual comparison includes objects of similar shape that ultimately received different identifications. A
perforated disc, for example, may be a spindle whorl, bead, gaming piece, or unfinished blank depending on
mass, wear, material, and find context. The catalogue keeps rejected alternatives in the notes when they were
seriously considered, which makes specialist reasoning visible and prevents the final label from looking
inevitable.

## Final record-quality note

The specialist register also preserves objects that remained unidentified after study. Their dimensions,
material observations, and provenance are retained without assigning a function simply to complete the
catalogue. This matters because ambiguous pieces can become useful comparanda when a better-preserved example
is found in a later season. The report therefore treats 'unidentified' as a legitimate analytical outcome and
records why more specific alternatives were rejected.
