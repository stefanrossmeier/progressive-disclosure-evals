# Isotopic Pilot Study of Cemetery Individuals

## Scope and record history

Recording follows the excavation sheets, skeletal inventory, and specialist observations. Grave architecture,
body position, disturbance, and associated objects are treated separately so that biological interpretation is
not confused with burial practice. The grave cut and fill were recorded independently from the skeleton.
Disturbance by animals, later graves, and root action was mapped before lifting so that missing elements were
not automatically interpreted as ancient removal.

## Recording and analytical procedure

Age estimation combines dental development or wear with available skeletal indicators. Sex estimation is
reported probabilistically where preservation permits, and no estimate is made from grave goods. Pathology is
described before diagnosis. Healed fractures, joint change, dental disease, and non-specific stress markers
are recorded in a way that allows later reassessment without relying on a single interpretive label.

During archive preparation, drawings, photographs, sample logs, and object registers were reconciled against
the written narrative. Not every recorded observation contributes to the interpretation: colour changes at
baulk edges, isolated body sherds, displaced stones, minor root disturbance, and uncertain joins are retained
because they document the actual evidentiary texture of excavation. Coordinates and measurements were checked
for internal consistency, but values were not normalized merely to make neighbouring records agree. Where the
team used a shorthand description in the field, the expanded wording appears in the discussion while the
original identifier is preserved. This makes the archive suitable for later reanalysis without requiring the
reader to assume that the first interpretation was final.

## Detailed observations

Associated objects are listed by find number and position relative to the body. Their cultural or
chronological significance belongs primarily to the relevant specialist catalogues, which are cross-referenced
where necessary. The archive entry retains minor measurements, negative observations, and rejected joins
because these often matter when a later specialist revisits the material.

## Supplementary observations and limitations

Skeletal completeness differs markedly between graves. Acidic pockets, salt crystallization, root action, and
later cuts can preferentially destroy small or cancellous elements, so absence is not automatically
meaningful. Element inventories were checked after cleaning and refitting, and fragments whose side or element
could not be established remain in an indeterminate category. Measurements used for stature or biological
profile were taken only on sufficiently complete elements. The report retains excluded measurements in notes
when the reason for exclusion may matter to future reassessment.

Burial treatment is described before broader social interpretation. Body orientation, flexion, grave depth,
container traces, marker placement, and object position can vary independently, and the sample is too small to
assume each combination represents a fixed social category. Reopening and disturbance are considered where
articulated and disarticulated elements coexist. When grave goods appear displaced, the report distinguishes
movement caused by decomposition, later intrusion, and excavation damage as far as the evidence permits. These
cautions are especially important when comparing individual graves to cemetery-wide patterns.

Comparisons among graves use the same descriptive variables even when preservation differs, allowing absence,
uncertainty, and non-observation to remain distinct. A grave with no recorded vessel is not automatically
coded as a vessel-free burial if the upper part of the cut was destroyed. Likewise, demographic summaries
distinguish individuals that could not be aged or sexed from those assigned broad estimates. These conventions
make cemetery-level patterns slower to summarize but materially more reliable.

## Register extracts and secondary observations

entries, and 10 drawing or measurement checks. The reconciliation produced several minor observations that
were retained even though they did not alter the main interpretation: root etching is concentrated on the
upper surfaces of the long bones and is treated as post-depositional; small hand and foot bones are
underrepresented where the grave edge was disturbed by a later cut; and sediment staining marks the former
position of an organic element that did not itself survive. These details are deliberately kept in the

## Additional analytical caution

Individual biological profiles are kept separate from grave treatment because the two evidence streams have
different uncertainties. Age and sex estimates depend on preservation and available indicators, while grave
architecture and object placement may be altered by reopening or later cuts. The report therefore avoids
turning a single burial into a demographic type and instead documents the observations needed for later
cemetery-level comparison.

## Final record-quality note

Grave inventories were reconciled with lifting sheets after laboratory study because refitting can change the
apparent number of skeletal fragments and associated objects. When an item listed in the field could not be
matched to a surviving bag or photograph, the discrepancy remains explicit rather than being silently
resolved. This preserves the difference between excavation observation, later laboratory confirmation, and
archival uncertainty, which is essential for reliable cemetery comparisons.
